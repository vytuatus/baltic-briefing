Key reporting

The stories that matter today, each with the type of evidence behind it and a critical take.

Unidentified object enters eastern Latvia from the Russia–Belarus border area

Official statement, on the record Latvian armed forces, relayed by Reuters, the Kyiv Independent and Izvestia; one original source.

On the evening of 1 November Latvia's armed forces warned of a threat in the airspace over the Krāslava and Ludza municipalities, near the borders with Belarus and Russia, and told residents to stay indoors. The alert lasted from about 18:59 to 19:30 local time; the military said the object left Latvian airspace across the Russian border. The Kyiv Independent reports that Latvia launched a BLAZE interceptor drone and that the object was likely a drone, but its origin is not yet clear.

Critical take. The facts are thin. Earlier stray drones over Latvia have included Ukrainian drones pushed off course, reportedly by Russian jamming, and Russian ones, so neither origin should be assumed. Izvestia's headline calls it a drone and its related links point to Ukrainian drones, which goes further than the Latvian statement. A quick alert and interceptor launch show that Latvia's air-defence procedures are working.

NATO's top commander: Russia threatens NATO, but conflict is not inevitable

Official statement, on the record LSM interview, reported by Izvestia; consistent with his earlier public remarks in June and October.

In an interview with Latvian public media on 30 October, General Alexus Grynkewich, NATO's Supreme Allied Commander Europe, said Russia poses a threat to the alliance but he does not consider conflict inevitable, so NATO must make sure it is ready. He praised Latvia for reaching defence spending of 5% of GDP ahead of schedule and for its work on societal resilience.

Critical take. Russian state media presented the interview as NATO preparing for conflict and placed it next to Putin's line that a war with Europe would have no winners. Grynkewich's actual message, readiness as a way to prevent war, matches the Lithuanian baseline: a low near-term threat and a longer-term risk that needs deterrence.

Estonia debates whether its warnings about Russia have become too predictable

Commentary ERR, 31 October and 1 November.

Former diplomat Jaap Ora, now a member of the opposition Isamaa party, told ERR that Estonia's Russia message has become predictable and risks partner fatigue, and that public statements should be measured. Prime Minister Kristen Michal said he will not hold back on Russia, and the outgoing foreign ministry secretary general said Estonia should never consider giving up its voice.

Critical take. This is fallout from Reuters' anonymous-source report that the Pentagon asked the Baltic states to tone down their rhetoric. It is a domestic argument about style and influence, not new evidence about Russian intentions or about the request itself, which remains contested.

Satellite imagery shows a large Russian radar under construction in Kaliningrad

Imagery or documents ICEYE imagery via FAZ and Stars and Stripes; also discussed by the Robert Lansing Institute (commentary). No Russian or NATO official comment.

Imagery analysed by the satellite company ICEYE and reported by FAZ and Stars and Stripes on 21 October shows a large over-the-horizon radar being built near Gvardeysk, about 37 km from the Polish border, with three antenna arrays each roughly 500 m long. ICEYE also counted more than 20 signals-intelligence and electronic-warfare sites across the exclave.

Critical take. Added as a catch-up because earlier editions missed it. The construction is well documented, but claimed ranges of around 3,000 km are projections, and it is not known whether the radar works. It fits the baseline's judgement that Russia keeps building long-term military capacity, but a radar is a sensor, not a sign of imminent attack.

European intelligence officials say Russia targets defence-industry people and supply chains

Anonymous sources FT and FAZ reporting, summarised by Al Jazeera; the intelligence officials are not named.

An Al Jazeera overview on 29 October brings together reports that representatives of five EU intelligence agencies told the Financial Times that Russia wants to kill European defence executives and disrupt their supply chains, and that FAZ reported incendiary devices planted in offices of two defence-technology firms in Munich. It also recalls the August plot against cargo planes at Leipzig/Halle, which the European Commission attributed to Russia.

Critical take. The overview collects earlier reports rather than breaking new cases, so nothing here is counted as a new sabotage case. The pattern fits the baseline's warnings about preparation for disruptive operations, but the claim of plans to kill executives rests on unnamed officials.

How this briefing works

Source priority

  • Official and intelligence: Baltic, Nordic, German, UK and US services, NATO and defence ministries.
  • Quality journalism: Baltic broadcasters, Ukrainian outlets, Reuters, AP and major international papers.
  • Analysts and open-source investigators: ISW, RUSI, satellite-imagery analysts and incident trackers.
  • The Russian side: independent Russian outlets, plus state media to see the Kremlin's narrative.
  • Commentators and influencers: included, always labelled as commentary rather than evidence.

Evidence labels

  • Official statement: on the record, attributable.
  • Imagery or documents: something you can inspect.
  • Anonymous sources: credible outlet, not verifiable.
  • Unverified claim: a single or partisan source.

Many outlets repeating one wire report is one source, not many.

Limits

Built from the public web only: no dark-web, classified or paywalled material. Intelligence claims are reported as stated, not independently verified. Troop and logistics movements are only visible when someone publishes them, so a quiet indicator means nothing has been reported, not that nothing is happening. Not a substitute for official warnings from your government.
